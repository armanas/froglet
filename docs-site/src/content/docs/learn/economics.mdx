## The trust model, layer by layer

Froglet separates three kinds of trust. Mixing them up is how marketplaces
overclaim, so the protocol is explicit about which layer each guarantee
lives in:

| Checkable evidence | Operator policy (hosted marketplace) | Not guaranteed by the protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Artifact signatures (BIP340) | Listing and indexing | Execution **correctness** |
| SHA-256 hashes and the chain | Suspension via the [arbiter](#disputes-today-the-arbiter) | Provider competence |
| Lightning settlement state (payment preimage) | Complaint handling | Refund of the base fee on failure |
| Signing-key continuity (not proof of the same person) | Identity attestation (DNS / OAuth domain claims) | Future behavior of any key |

### Trust signals today

The marketplace API defines a **receipt-derived trust summary** per provider:
success count, failure count, rejected/canceled count, and total settled value,
computed from the signed receipts the indexer has verified. Two honest caveats:

- **Receipt history can be self-dealt.** A provider can run deals against
  itself to inflate counts. Counts and reported settled totals do not prove
  independent buyers or a real economic cost, especially for free or test
  deals. Weigh history together with other evidence.
- **Attestation supplies context, not quality assurance.** A verified domain
  claim links a key to control of that domain. It does not prove a unique
  person, good results, or a meaningful cost to creating another identity.

### Disputes today: the arbiter

The hosted marketplace's documented arbiter accepts complaints with evidence;
operator verdicts can suspend a provider from marketplace listings. This is an
external service, whose availability must be checked with its operator.

:::caution[Operator-adjudicated, by design — for now]
The MVP arbiter is **operator-adjudicated**. It is not decentralized,
stake-backed, panel-selected, or slashable. Suspension hides a provider from
the hosted marketplace's read API; it does not invalidate signed artifacts and
it is not a protocol-level rule. Deposits, public adjudicator registration,
appeals, panels, and slashing are post-MVP work. See
[ARBITER.md](https://github.com/armanas/froglet/blob/main/docs/ARBITER.md) for
the full contract.
:::

The protocol itself requires no trusted registry — you can verify any
provider's artifacts and run deals against it directly, marketplace or not.
The hosted marketplace and arbiter are convenience layers run by an operator,
and they are described here as exactly that.

### What a receipt proves — and what it doesn't

A valid receipt signature establishes that the provider key signed that
reported outcome and result hash. Checking the chain also requires the linked
artifacts, their signatures, and their hash bindings; a receipt alone is not
the whole chain. For Lightning, a matching preimage is settlement evidence
when checked against the deal's bound payment hash and invoice.

A receipt does **not** prove the result is *correct*. Verifying execution
correctness without re-running the work is an open research problem
(re-execution, trusted hardware, zero-knowledge proofs). Froglet's position:
the receipt makes outcomes *attributable and auditable* — a provider that
signs wrong results signs the evidence against itself — but detection still
requires someone to check. For deterministic workloads, spot-check by
re-running a sample on a second provider.

### Practical buyer guidance

- Keep individual deal sizes small relative to the trust evidence you have.
- Configure your node's requester spend caps so a runaway agent can't drain a
  wallet (see [Configuration](https://github.com/armanas/froglet/blob/main/docs/CONFIGURATION.md)).
- In the **two-leg Lightning model**, the **base fee is non-refundable** if execution fails — that is the
  provider's compensation for reserved resources. Only the success fee is
  conditional. Size base fees accordingly when you buy.
- Lightning payments are irreversible; Stripe payments follow card-network
  dispute rules. Pick the rail that matches your recourse expectations.

## Design roadmap: staked identity

The long-term design adds a fourth trust signal: a **non-refundable identity
cost**, called stake in the roadmap. The intended signal is a verifiable
payment associated with a provider key. Losing a listing could make that
investment less useful. This is not a refundable bond, a guarantee of honest
behavior, or a way to invalidate the provider's signed artifacts.

:::note[Status: designed, not live]
The stake schema and endpoints exist in the marketplace codebase but are
**disabled pending settlement-backed stake receipts** — a stake must be proven
by a verifiable payment before it counts. Nothing below is a live guarantee
yet.
:::

The design only works if three assumptions hold, and we state them plainly:

1. **Cheating must be detectable and publicly provable.** For "provider never
   delivered," receipts and deadlines make this checkable. For "provider
   returned a wrong result," it requires re-execution or verifiable compute —
   see the receipt caveat above.
2. **Someone must adjudicate.** An identity cost cannot detect cheating or
   decide a dispute. Today the adjudicator would be the marketplace operator;
   any panel-based mechanism is future work.
3. **Exposure is concurrent.** A cheating provider can run many deals in
   parallel before detection propagates, so per-deal arithmetic alone
   understates risk. The relevant comparison is stake versus *total concurrent
   exposure within the detection window*, which is why deal-size caps and
   small-first defaults matter even with stake.

With those assumptions, a requester heuristic falls out: require
`stake / exposure > k` for a risk threshold `k` of your choosing — where
`exposure` is everything you have outstanding with that provider, not just
this deal.

## Design note: the deal as a game

This simplified model describes **two-leg Lightning settlement**, not every
payment rail. It assumes risk-neutral participants, a provider cost $c$ for
every admitted attempt, and no extra transaction costs. Here, $q$ is the
probability of an accepted successful outcome. It combines execution and
requester acceptance; it is not an independently measured quality score.
See [Settlement](/learn/settlement/) for implementation maturity.

### Definitions

Let $\mathcal{N}$ be the set of all froglet nodes. Each node $n \in \mathcal{N}$ has a secp256k1 keypair $(k_n, P_n)$ where $P_n = k_n \cdot G$.

A **service** $s$ is characterized by:

$$s = (\text{offer\_kind}, \text{runtime}, b_s, f_s, \mathbf{L}_s)$$

where $b_s$ is the base fee, $f_s$ is the success fee, and $\mathbf{L}_s = (t_{\max}, m_{\max}, \phi_{\max})$ is the execution limit vector (time, memory, fuel).

A deal between requester $R$ and provider $P$ is a two-player sequential game:

$$\text{Quote} \xrightarrow{\text{P signs}} \text{Deal} \xrightarrow{\text{R signs}} \text{Execute} \xrightarrow{\text{P runs}} \text{Receipt} \xrightarrow{\text{P signs}}$$

### Payoffs

Let $v$ be the value to the requester, $c$ the provider's cost, $p = b_s + f_s$ the total price.

| Outcome | Requester | Provider |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Success | $v - p$ | $p - c$ |
| Failure | $-b_s$ | $b_s - c$ |
| No deal | $0$ | $0$ |

Note the failure row: the requester eats the base fee. That is deliberate
(it compensates reserved resources and deters free-riding) and it is why the
base fee should stay small relative to the success fee.

### Participation constraints

Using expected payoffs, the requester participates when $qv \geq b_s + qf_s$,
and the provider when $b_s + qf_s \geq c$. Positive expected gains from trade
require $qv > c$. At $q=1$, these reduce to the familiar successful-deal bounds.

### Two-leg settlement analysis

Let $q \in [0, 1]$ be the probability of the accepted success defined above.

**Requester expected payoff:**

$$\mathbb{E}[\pi_R] = q(v - b_s - f_s) + (1-q)(-b_s) = qv - b_s - qf_s$$

**Provider expected payoff:**

$$\mathbb{E}[\pi_P] = q(b_s + f_s - c) + (1-q)(b_s - c) = b_s + qf_s - c$$

### Incentive compatibility

Holding cost and all other terms fixed, a positive success fee increases
expected revenue as $q$ increases:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[\pi_P]}{\partial q} = f_s > 0$$

This is a marginal revenue incentive, not an equilibrium or honesty proof.
If improving reliability also increases cost, that cost must be included.

### What a fee split can guarantee

There is no unique optimal split from these assumptions alone. Breaking even
on **every failed attempt** requires:

$$b_s \geq c$$

Breaking even **in expectation** requires $b_s + qf_s \geq c$. A smaller base
fee shifts more failure risk to the provider. For example, with $c=2$ sat,
$b_s=1$ sat, $f_s=4$ sat, and $q=0.5$, expected provider profit is $1$ sat,
but each failed attempt loses $1$ sat. These are different guarantees.

## Network economics

### Marketplace viability

Suppose a marketplace charges $(b_M, f_M)$ per search. With query rate $\lambda$ and $N_P$ indexed providers:

$$\text{Rev}_M = \lambda \cdot (b_M + q_M f_M)$$

$$C_M = c_{\text{idx}} \cdot N_P + c_{\text{qry}} \cdot \lambda$$

Viable when $\text{Rev}_M > C_M$. (The current hosted marketplace charges
nothing — this models how an operator could sustain one.)

### Provider discovery value

A provider's expected value from marketplace registration:

$$\Delta V_P = \lambda_M \cdot (b_P + q_P f_P - c_P) - \text{registration cost}$$

Here $\lambda_M$ is the number of additional admitted deals from discovery in
the period, with the same per-attempt cost assumption. Registration has a
positive expected return when $\Delta V_P > 0$.

### Network effects

As a possible model of cross-side network effects, a steady state would satisfy:

$$N_P^* = g_P(N_R^*), \quad N_R^* = g_R(N_P^*)$$

Even if $g_P$ and $g_R$ are increasing, the existence and number of equilibria
depend on their shape and boundary conditions. Growth is not guaranteed by
the protocol.

## Welfare analysis

Total welfare:

$$\mathbb{E}[W] = \sum_{\text{deals}} (q_i v_i - c_i) - \text{transaction costs}$$

Within this model, fees are transfers between participants. Potential benefits
must outweigh execution and transaction costs. The design aims to help through:

1. **Reducing information asymmetry** — signed receipts make outcomes attributable
2. **Reducing counterparty risk on settlement** — two-leg Lightning binds the success fee to acceptance
3. **Reducing search costs** — the marketplace aggregates information
4. **Enabling price discovery** — comparable terms can help buyers compare providers

Market size alone does not imply cost-level prices or improved welfare.

:::tip[The honest economic conclusion]
Froglet makes commitments and outcomes *signed, attributable, and auditable*,
and binds the two-leg Lightning success fee to acceptance cryptographically. Execution
correctness and provider quality remain market problems — addressed today by
receipt-derived signals, attestation, small-deal discipline, and an
operator-adjudicated arbiter; addressed tomorrow, if the roadmap holds, by
settlement-backed stake.
:::
